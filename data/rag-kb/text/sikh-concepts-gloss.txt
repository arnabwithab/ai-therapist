# Sikh concepts for counselling (gloss)

Provenance: in-repo gloss file (F021). Summaries in plain English for
counselling use; no scripture quoted at length. Offer one idea at a time,
as an option, never a prescription.

## Naam simran (remembrance)

The practice of turning the mind back, again and again, to the Name — through
quiet repetition or meditation. Counselling gloss: rumination pulls the mind
toward threat and regret; simran is the trained opposite — gently returning
attention to one steady anchor. Clients who already do paath or simran can be
encouraged to notice its calming effect and use it deliberately at anxious
moments, the way others use paced breathing.

## Sangat (company of the good)

Wellbeing is shaped by the company one keeps; the congregation uplifts.
Counselling gloss: isolation deepens depression, and community protects.
Ask about their sangat — family, friends, gurdwara community — and treat
reconnection as a behavioural goal, not just advice.

## Seva (selfless service)

Serving others without expectation — in the gurdwara, the neighbourhood, the
family. Counselling gloss: depression narrows life to the self and its pain;
seva reverses the flow. Behavioural activation by another name: one small act
of usefulness a week can break the inertia that talk alone cannot.

## Chardi kala (rising spirits)

The Sikh ideal of eternal optimism — high spirits maintained even in hardship.
Counselling gloss: not denial of pain but refusal to be defined by it. For a
client crushed by a setback: acknowledge the wound fully first, then ask what
chardi kala would look like for them — what is the smallest step taken in
high spirits?

## Hukam (acceptance of what is)

Living in alignment with the Divine order — accepting what cannot be changed
while acting where one can. Counselling gloss: the serenity distinction —
grief, diagnosis, loss — meets resistance ("why me?"). Hukam reframes
acceptance not as surrender but as dignity: bow to what is, then move.

## The five vices (panj vikar)

Kam (lust), krodh (anger), lobh (greed), moh (attachment), ahankar (ego-pride).
Counselling gloss: an indigenous map of the patterns that hijack behaviour.
A client stuck in anger outbursts or compulsive grasping may recognise
themselves faster in "krodh has hold of me" than in a clinical label — and
recognition is the first step to regulation.
