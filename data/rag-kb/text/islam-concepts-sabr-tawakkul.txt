# Islamic concepts for counselling: sabr and tawakkul (gloss)

Provenance: in-repo gloss file (F021). Plain-English summaries for counselling
use. Offer one idea at a time, as an option, never a prescription.

## Sabr (patient endurance)

Sabr is not passive waiting — it is steadfastness: holding to what is right
while difficulty passes, restraining the impulse to despair or to act rashly.
Counselling gloss: clients often believe distress must be eliminated
immediately, and panic when it is not. Sabr reframes endurance itself as
strength and worship: "you do not have to fix this feeling tonight; you only
have to carry it faithfully." Pairs with distress-tolerance skills — the
feeling is endured, not obeyed.

## Tawakkul (trustful reliance)

To strive with one's full effort and then entrust the outcome to Allah —
"t Tie your camel, then trust." Counselling gloss: anxiety lives in the gap
between effort and outcome. Tawakkul divides that gap cleanly: your share is
the effort (study, treatment, the difficult conversation); the outcome was
never yours to control. This directly counters outcome anxiety and the
illusion that more worrying equals more control.

## Shukr (gratitude) as attention training

Deliberate remembrance of blessings, in ease and in hardship. Counselling
gloss: depression trains attention on what is missing. A small daily shukr
practice — naming three specific mercies — is gratitude journaling in the
client's own idiom, and therefore more likely to be sustained.

## Dua as coping, not bargaining

Turning to Allah in supplication externalises the burden: the problem is
voiced, witnessed, and handed to the Most Merciful. Counselling gloss:
encourage dua as emotional expression and connection, not as a transaction
("if I pray enough, the problem must vanish"). When dua goes unanswered,
normalise the grief rather than letting it curdle into "Allah has abandoned
me" — that turn is the spiritual-struggle pattern and needs gentle handling.

## Mercy larger than sin

"Do not despair of the mercy of Allah" (39:53, paraphrase). Guilt over past
actions is one of the commonest pains Muslim clients bring. Counselling gloss:
guilt that leads to repair (apology, change, istighfar) is healthy; guilt
that loops endlessly into self-punishment is not repentance but rumination.
The tradition's own emphasis on mercy is the lever: a merciful Lord does not
ask for endless self-torture.
