A BSTRACT Background: A multidimensional family support and well-being programme (FSWP) was initiated to support the families of children in conflict with law (CICL) and strengthen their participation in the integration process. This programme aims to successfully reintegrate children into the family and build parental capacity to manage them. This study presents the overview of the multidimensional FSWP initiated in an observation home, facility for CICLs, Bengaluru, a metropolitan city of India. Methods and Materials: The team of psychiatric social workers systematically delivered the family support programme to promote families’ involvement at multiple levels (individual, relationships, community, and societal) for successful reintegration of children in communities. The preliminary data about the participants was collected using strengths and difficulties questionnaire and parent interview schedule. Results: The programme activities consisted of engaging parents and family members in the parenting management training programme, addressing their psychosocial issues, identifying resources for postrelease rehabilitation and promotive interventions with children and their parents. The FSWP activities are developed to increase positive outcomes like favorable behavioral changes in children and emotional regulation, frequent participation and support of parents in the trial and rehabilitation process, and encouraging parental involvement in for successful community reintegration and placements of children. Conclusion: The family characteristics are integrally related to delinquency and practioners need to integrate such factors to improve parenting behaviors and enhance positive relationships between families and children. Introduction The psychosocial issues of children in conflict with law (CICL) are complex and multidimensional. During the year of 2017–2018, a total of 38,256 juveniles were apprehended in 31,591 cases, under Indian Penal Code and Special and Local Laws out of which the majority of children (75.5%) were in the age group of 16–18 years.[] Studies suggest that familial factors, such as poor parental management, broken families, absence of parental involvement, inadequate supervision and monitoring, and parental inconsistency, are frequently considered hazardous for upcoming delinquency or crime among children.[] Consequently, the children in conflict with the law are at higher risk for developing mental health issues. It is estimated that as high as 70% of the youths in conflict with law have a diagnosable mental health problem and the common ones are externalizing disorders, that is, conduct disorder, ADHD, substance use disorder, and internalizing disorders such as anxiety and depression.[,] The international protective treaties and conventions, such as the convention on the rights of the child, states that no child can be deprived of their liberty unlawfully and state nations must act in the children’s best interest.[] Over the decades, the child rights organizations such as The United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), the Human Rights Watch (HRW), the Defense for Children International (DCI) and so forth, have emphasized ensuring the protection of the rights of children in conflict with the law.[] Globally, the number of CICL staying in correctional settings and observation homes with limited education opportunities and other services continues to grow rapidly. After their release from correctional homes, the children face significant hurdles in reintegrating or rehabilitating in the communities. The prospects for getting a job or other psychosocial services and maintaining them are very small.[] Children in conflict with the law often remain unemployed not by their own choice and have higher risks to return back to criminal behaviors. A substantial body of research has shown that psychosocial services such as rehabilitation programmes successfully reduce recidivism, particularly when provided in institutions before the release of children.[] Thus, it could be valuable to provide interventions to children and families before their release from correctional settings.[] Social workers have a long history of working with children in conflict with the law and their families to curb juvenile delinquency and facilitate prevention and rehabilitation. Recent studies have found the need to develop interventions at individual, family, and community levels to prevent juvenile crimes as children do not understand the consequences of offenses.[] Parental involvement in the juvenile justice system is now widely recognized as a crucial element in promoting positive child outcomes in education, mental health, child welfare, and juvenile justice.[] Literature suggests that community-based and family-focused interventions (e.g., multisystemic therapy)[] appear to decrease adolescent behavioral problems and delinquent behavior compared to individual treatment.[] It is also a well-known fact that parent–child relationship, whether positive or negative, is a significant factor in child development. Healthy, stable, and intact families play a crucial role in children’s socialization, enabling them to become helpful society members. Although there is extensive literature on the predictors and risk factors associated with conduct disorder and delinquency, there is little on the effectiveness of prevention and intervention.[] Moreover, the methods for enhancing parental involvement in the care of CICL are still under-developed.[] Now withstanding the evidence, there is a lack of active and positive parental involvement in the juvenile justice system, particularly in the Indian context. With this need, this study describes the Family Support and Well-being Programme (FSWP) services rendered by the team for parents and families of CICL. FSWP has been planned for the parents of CICL in the observation home by the psychiatric social work team associated with the child and adolescent psychiatry unit of tertiary mental health center in Bengaluru. The overall focus of FSWP is to encourage family involvement in the successful reintegration and rehabilitation of children in difficult circumstances. This report aims to provide details of FSWP services to improve parenting behaviors and enhance positive relationships between families and CICL. Methods and Materials 
 FSWP: Concept and Essential Features The FSWP were started in August 2019 by the Department of Psychiatric Social Work from a tertiary mental health care center in South India, in association with the MAANASA project initiated by the Juvenile Justice Board, Bengaluru Urban. The permission and approval was taken from the NIMHANS under registration number: NIMH/COORD/PSW/KVJ/963/2019-20 dated 26-09-2019. The FSWP is aimed at providing multidimensional psychosocial services (individual counseling, parent interventions, recreational and educational activities and rehabilitation programs and parent-focused interventions) to successfully reintegrate CICL with their families. The psychiatric social work team provides such services weekly by visiting the observation home for CICL. The FSWP comprises a detailed psychosocial assessment using a validated psychosocial assessment proforma and standardized instruments, development of care plan based on the assessments and providing interventions at various levels []. The essential interventions of the FSWP at various levels are: 
 i) Interventions at individual level- A detailed assessment of child’s environment, child’s emotional and behavioral difficulties, psychosocial needs of the children, social support and resources of parents and families. The interventions with children consisted of individual counseling, skill training and promotive interventions such as life skills education. ii) Interventions at family level: Detailed family and psychosocial assessment; and understanding parental/familial needs. The family interventions consisted of parent management training (PMT); group work with families; psychoeducation to the parents; family social support system strengthening strategies; family guidance services; stress management; and strengthening capacities of families and parents to care for CICL. iii) Rehabilitative and reintegrative interventions: Determining education and vocational needs of the children, assessment of family resources for rehabilitation of children and proposing a vocational or educational plan for CICL based on the assessment. iv) Awareness creation sessions on themes relating to mental health: Structured and semistructured programs on themes like substance use, peer influences, preventive and promotive aspects of mental health, importance of personal grooming and hygiene, managing conflicts in relationships, adjusting to life in custodial conditions and safety measures, and so on. 
 Participants and Permission This study presents data of the 72 parents of CICL seen under the FSWP from August 2019 till March 2020. The PSW team collected the data from the parents of CICL using psychosocial assessment proforma, strengths and difficulties questionnaire (SDQ) P17-parent version and parenting interview schedule. Prior permission was sought from Institute NIMHANS, Bengaluru to initiate the Family Support Programme in Government Observation Home, Bengaluru, under the MAANASA project of Juvenile Justice Board Bengaluru. Procedure and instrument details Procedure Parents of the CICL were referred by the Member of the Juvenile Justice Board. After their consent to be part of the program, basic socio-demographic details are collected from the parents/caregivers. The team conducted a detailed interviews with the parents to gather the information on various psychosocial concerns. Instrument Details 
 i) Psychosocial Assessment Proforma The PSW Team developed the semistructured interview schedule which consisted of information about the index child, family dynamics including parenting factors, socio-environmental factors and protection and rehabilitation needs of children. ii) SDQ P-17 (Parent Version) The SDQ is a 25-item questionnaire for child and adolescent mental health problems. It is for 4–17-year-old children and adolescents and is used for clinical assessment, epidemiological study and screening of psychiatric disorders. The 25-items are answered using a 3-point scale, “certainly true,” “somewhat true,” and “not true” and scored from 0 to 2 points. The items yield five subscale scores that range from 0 to 10 including: (1) emotional symptoms; (2) conduct symptoms; (3) hyperactivity/inattention; (4) peer relationship problems; and (5) prosocial behavior. Summing emotional, conduct, hyperactivity/inattention and peer relationship subscale scores yields a total difficulty score that ranges from 0 to 40. The SDQ uses cut-off scores that are defined using normative data banding in three categories: normal (80 th percentile and less), borderline (80 th –90 th percentile), or abnormal (90 th percentile and more).[] iii) Parent Interview Schedule (WHO, 1990) It is a semistructured interview developed by World Health Organization[] to study psychosocial problems in children and adolescents and it has been primarily to assess the deviance or disturbance in the child’s family and environment. The schedule provides information on intrafamilial communication, upbringing qualities, the immediate environment’s nature, life events, societal stress, and interpersonal stress at home, school, or work. 
 Statistical Analysis The data collected from 72 parents were analyzed using descriptive statistics such as frequency, mean and Standard deviation and percentages. Statistical Package for Social Sciences software (SPSS for Windows, 22.0 IBM Crop., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyze the data.

Figure 2 Family support programme rehabilitation and reintegration services Discussion The study highlights the nature of the novel intervention programme that emphasized the family strengthening psychosocial services provided for CICL. This study also examined the mental health and psychosocial issues of 72 children who had come in conflict with law. It highlights the services prepared for parents to prevent and control children’s behavioral problems with appropriate skills and competencies through the program interventions. The following psychosocial findings of this study can be better understood from the socioecological perspective that influences the delinquent behaviors in these children.[] It emphasizes that apart from the child’s temperament, interactions with the unfavorable contextual factors such as sociodemographic and family environment will increase the risk of delinquency. Influence of sociodemographic factors on juvenile delinquency The sociodemographic profile of the parents indicated that more than half of them belonged to lower socioeconomic category. This is similar to the findings from literature citing that children coming from low-SES families have a higher tendency to engage in delinquent behavior than children from higher SES families.[] Additionally, the study by Birckhead has shown that an inability of the family to afford certain essential commodities such as food and clothing has significantly contributed to juvenile delinquency.[] Overwhelmingly, the majority of the parents of the CICL are from urban areas as nonconformity behavior of peer friendship, urban poverty and comparative deficiency, absence of selfcontrol, parental administration, and easy right to use illegal ways are related to juvenile crimes.[] However, it should be noted that relative to urban crime, some research has suggested that rural crime is also a growing concern.[] The parental status of children found that ninety percent children had been under the care of both parents, contrary to the literature citing children from divorced or one-parent families are likely found engaged in criminal and antisocial behaviors, unemployed and drop out of schools.[] However, parenting practices and other socioeconomic disadvantages could be associated or influenced. Emotional and behavioral issues and juvenile delinquency Regarding the emotional and behavioral issues in these children, consistent with existing literature,[,] the findings showed higher rates of SDQ variables such as hyperactivity problems, conduct problems, emotional issues, and antisocial attitudes. The majority of the children were found to have issues with peers or involved in the defiant peer association which is often related to juvenile delinquency and emotional and behavioral issues.[] Family, parenting practices and juvenile delinquency The findings of the PIS found presence of significant family and parenting stressors such as parental substance use, mental illness, poor supervision and monitoring, poor parenting practices, and family imprisonment. This could have been the reasons for these parents’ referral to FSWP as negative experiences, including physical and sexual abuse, rejection and neglect from society, broken homes, impoverished financial status, domestic violence, and so forth are often to juvenile delinquency.[] The study conducted by Chaffin reported that parents who instilled antisocial attitudes and behaviors in their children encouraged such attitudes to persist in them into adulthood,[] this supports the findings of adverse home environment conditions of children found on PIS in the current study. This study also discusses the relevance of the paradigm of family response in the FSWP services delivery in the context of juvenile delinquency. Studies have found that significant deprivation of various aspects of child development such as emotional, intellectual, social and physical domains incline children to engage in destructive choices later in the adulthood. For example, children with low emotional skills are likely to engage in criminal activities.[] Neurobiological deficits including poor executive and maladaptive functioning of the cortical and sub cortical regions are also responsible for such deviant behaviors.[] The other psychosocial causal factors, particularly the familial constructs such as lack of access to education due to low family income,[] drug abuse and associated compulsive economic behavior, parental separation, poor parent–child relationship, harsh or lax discipline, inadequate monitoring, supervision, low parental involvement, antisocial parents, broken home, low socioeconomic status, poverty, abusive parents, and family conflict are found as major contributors to juvenile delinquency.[] This can also be based on the social control theory[] that assumes that people are more likely to engage in delinquent behavior when social bonds erode. This theory held that the only way to restrain children from delinquency is to strengthen social bonds, especially involvement of the family, peers and school, and positive relationships.[] The family structure, positive relationships, and response patterns in addressing the needs of the children play an essential role in shaping their personality.[] This all happens to the child by providing security and developing their values and skills. Suppose the adverse familial conditions and practices can be a major factor responsible for juvenile delinquency and its adverse conditions. In that case, addressing the same hostile conditions and practices through family-focused interventions may be essential in preventing children from committing crimes.[] The FSWP The tenets of FSWP were therefore based on systematic intervention with families for prevention of crimes and successful reintegration of CICL. There is enough evidence suggesting that interventions involving working with families,[,] skill-building training, and enhancing positive relationships effectively deal with the problem of juvenile delinquency.[] Majority of the parents were provided individual counseling and psychoeducation interventions. The main focus was to address individual concerns and help parents to bring back their positive copings. The focus was also to provide them with information about the adolescent challenges and issues, psychosocial stressors related with crimes, neurobiological factors and need of proper management. These interventions have been found to be effective in preventing the child delinquency and developing resilience among parents to cope with the adversities.[] With an emphasis on parenting and family-oriented services, FSWP incorporated few services focusing on enhancing parenting skills and addressing maladaptive patterns in the families, which often remain factors for child recidivism.[,] Parental work included explaining parents’ goal setting, differential reinforcements, effective communication, and problem-solving skills. These parent-specific interventions have been found very effective in reducing the delinquent behaviors of children in other studies.[] The FSWP also held group sessions with the parents to facilitate the parents to discuss prevention of future problem behaviors, experience sharing and learnings, encouraging parents to take leadership roles, and conflict resolution. Such interventions were also found effective by various research studies in reducing juvenile crimes around the globe.[,] Last, the FSWP also included the component of life skill education with focus on promoting well-being, selfempowerment, information on risk-taking behaviors and skills such as communication, assertiveness, selfawareness, decision-making, problem-solving, critical and creative thinking. In India, life skills education have been implemented in schools, but this concept has not been much introduced in the juvenile justice system as a preventive programme.[] Studies have found life skills education as a proactive tool in addressing juvenile delinquency and helped the families manage the deviated and children with high risk-taking behaviors.[] The goal of life skills education in FSWP is to make individuals understand the legal and social consequences of violent behaviors and control such impulses. Therefore, the FSWP services comprehensively focused on the need for involvement of the family in administering reformative justice for these children. The strength of this study is that the information provided in this study will add to the knowledge of practitioners in this field. This programme is first of its kind in India dealing with families and CICL. This report has not just provided details about the psychosocial issues of children but also describes in detail about the psychosocial interventions that can be provided to CICL. The programme has successfully shown that it is possible to work with the vulnerable and sensitive population of families and CICL which has largely been ignored in the Indian context. This model can be replicated in the other parts of the country. The limitation lies in the fact that the model was implemented in one of the urban district centers and is yet to be implemented and tested in other centers. The issues of other centers can be different and hence the model may require appropriate modifications. Therefore, the model requires further efficacy testing with other centers. Conclusion In conclusion, we can be fairly certain that family characteristics are integrally related to delinquency. These characteristics generally involve ineffectual family functioning, which is experienced as problematic by children. It is time to start taking the relationship between family characteristics and delinquency very seriously and systematically determine the precise mechanisms of how family characteristics are related to delinquency. This study presented details about the FSWP services to reduce juvenile delinquency. Such programmes are very rare in India due to varied reasons such as negative attitudes towards the CICL, lack of human resources, infrastructure, budget, and logistical reasons. The need of the hour is to build necessary scientific research and infrastructure by adapting work from other areas of practice to reflect the particular features of service involvement in juvenile justice. In addition to that, there is also a need to use longitudinal and primary data for collecting crucial in gaining a better understanding of the impact of family variables on delinquency and violent behaviors. Families are important councils of social regulation and socialization. Stable family structures to offer children secure households in which they can be taught and absorb constructive social values to reduce the rate of child delinquency.[] So, alongside other crucial groups in society, families can be more positively involved in the interventions targeted at CICLs. Future research should focus on studying various psychosocial factors influencing the CICL and developing interventions to alter parenting practices, promote better child socialization, and reduce engagement in negative outcomes. Last but not least, the social workers and allied professional need to encourage the formation and promotion of public policies that support the families of CICLs for their better rehabilitation and reintegration in the community.
